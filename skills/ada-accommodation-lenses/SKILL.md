# ADA Accommodation Lenses: Four Corners

Advisory only. This is general information, not legal advice. It flags, tags, and drafts; the user decides and sends. It contains no names, claim numbers, or health facts. Case facts come from the user in chat, never from this file.

## The four corners

Every document in an accommodation file is read by four seats. Each sees different things and misses different things. Read every document, form field, and draft from all four before it goes out, and name the corner when reporting a finding.

| Corner | Who sits there | What they read for | What they miss |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. ADA examiner | The administrator's case examiner or accommodation specialist | Completeness, provider certification, duration, the link from limitation to request | Legal duties beyond the form; anything outside the checklist |
| 2. Law and case law | A judge, an EEOC investigator, a lawyer on either side | Notice, the interactive process, essential functions, effectiveness, hardship, retaliation, deadlines | The practical "can we do this Monday" question |
| 3. HR | The employer's HR partner and the decision approver | Policy, precedent, confidentiality, essential functions as written, risk to the employer | The requester's lived limits; what the administrator's letters say |
| 4. Employee | The requester, and anyone who will later quote the file back at them | Their own words, their evidence, their weak points, their tone | How a neutral reader takes a sharp sentence |

A finding that holds from all four corners is solid. A finding that holds from one corner only is a flag, not a conclusion.

## Ground rules (learned in live use)

1. **Verify before building.** Claim status, closures, dates, and IDs get a screenshot or document before any message is drafted on them. Anything stated from memory is tagged RECALL.
2. **A description of a file is not the file.** A prep brief is not a provider letter. Ask to see it.
3. **Check form limits at the box.** Fields clip silently at their own caps (short boxes may hold about 100 characters, long ones about 300). Read the box's counter and the review page. Put a pointer first, keep the full answer in a supplement, and say in writing that fields were cut off.
4. **Use the user's own words about capability.** "Can function, not well" never becomes "unable." Overclaims get quoted back later.
5. **Do not add labels no clinician has documented.** Write "diagnoses documented to date include...," then describe function. Keep undocumented terms out until a provider writes them.
6. **Ask who reads each field** (administrator, employer, or both). Draft to the stated reader. Do not relabel fields or trim on a guess.
7. **Keep fallbacks inside the protections.** Offer alternatives that do not include what the provider ruled out.
8. **Split permanent from temporary** requests in the same text, so a temporary limit cannot expire the whole request.
9. **Request date is not effective date.** A start date earlier than the request can make an accommodation expire the day it is approved.
10. **Every fact carries its source and date.** If it would not go in a brief, it does not go in a message.
11. **No case is cited until it is verified.** Cases in `references/case-law.md` are a starter index tagged RECALL. Check each against the reporter or a citator for the user's circuit before it goes into anything sent.

## Corner 1: ADA examiner

The examiner runs the administrator's process. Usually they facilitate and the employer decides; confirm which, in writing, for this file.

- **Facilitator versus decider.** Who signs the decision letter? A portal status label ("approved," "closed," "pending") is not a decision letter. Ask for the letter.
- **Certification read.** The examiner checks that the provider form states: the impairment (or "documented to date"), the functional limitations, how each requested accommodation addresses a limitation, and the duration (permanent, temporary with end date, or to be reassessed). A gap in any one is the usual cause of a closure or a "pending information" status.
- **Closures for missing paperwork.** Record the closure date, the stated reason, what was requested and when, and whether a deadline was given in writing. A closure is often reopenable; ask how and by when.
- **Calls with no written record.** After any call, send a same-day recap: "Per our call today at [time], my understanding is... Please correct by [date] if I have this wrong." Ask for the examiner's call notes.
- **Naming drift.** Claim type and request type can be labeled differently on different screens and letters. Log each name with its source; ask which one governs.
- **What the examiner can do for the user.** Extend a paperwork deadline, reopen a closed request, route a question to the employer, confirm what the employer has been told.

## Corner 2: ADA law and case law

Statute and regulation are the frame; cases show how courts in the user's circuit apply it. See `references/case-law.md` for the starter index and verification steps.

- **Coverage.** Disability is construed broadly after the ADA Amendments Act of 2008 (Pub. L. 110-325); 42 U.S.C. 12102; 29 C.F.R. 1630.2(g)-(j). Mitigating measures are not considered, except ordinary eyeglasses or contact lenses. An episodic condition or one in remission counts if it would substantially limit when active.
- **Failure to accommodate.** 42 U.S.C. 12112(b)(5)(A). Elements generally: covered disability; qualified with or without accommodation; employer had notice and a request (no magic words); employer did not provide an effective accommodation.
- **Interactive process.** 29 C.F.R. 1630.2(o)(3) and the Appendix to Part 1630. Most circuits hold that a breakdown is not a standalone claim but is evidence when an accommodation was possible. Who caused the breakdown matters; the file should show the user responded promptly every time.
- **Essential functions.** 42 U.S.C. 12111(8); 29 C.F.R. 1630.2(n). The employer's judgment and any written job description prepared before advertising or interviewing are evidence, not the last word. Ask for the job description in force on the request date.
- **Effective, not preferred.** The employer may choose among effective accommodations (29 C.F.R. Part 1630 Appendix, 1630.9). No duty to create a new position, displace another employee, or remove an essential function. Reassignment to a vacant position is a recognized accommodation, limits vary by circuit.
- **Undue hardship.** 42 U.S.C. 12111(10); 29 C.F.R. 1630.2(p). The employer must show it, on the actual numbers and resources.
- **Medical inquiries.** 42 U.S.C. 12112(d); 29 C.F.R. 1630.14. Documentation requests must be job-related and limited to the disability and the need for accommodation.
- **Retaliation and interference.** 42 U.S.C. 12203(a) (retaliation for requesting or opposing) and 12203(b) (coercion, intimidation, threats, interference). Requesting an accommodation is protected activity.
- **Delegation.** Handing the process to an administrator does not by itself shield the employer from its own duties.
- **Deadlines.** An EEOC charge must be filed within 180 days of the act, or 300 days where a state or local fair employment agency covers the claim (42 U.S.C. 12117(a), adopting 42 U.S.C. 2000e-5(e)(1)). Confirm which applies for the user's state and flag the date in every timeline. A discrete denial starts its own clock.
- **Other law that may run alongside.** FMLA (leave, separate from ADA), state disability law (may be broader), Section 501 or 504 of the Rehabilitation Act (federal employers, federal contractors and fund recipients). Flag; do not analyze without the user's facts.

## Corner 3: HR

Read the file as the employer's HR partner and approver will read it.

- **Confidentiality.** Medical information is kept in separate confidential files (29 C.F.R. 1630.14(c)). Supervisors and managers may be told about work restrictions and accommodations, not diagnoses. A manager notice should carry the claim number and the approved restriction or accommodation, nothing else.
- **Need-to-know list.** Who has seen what, and when. A diagnosis reaching a manager or coworker is a flag.
- **Job description and precedent.** HR will check the written job description, what others in the role have been allowed (telework, schedule, equipment), and what this employee was allowed before the request. Prior informal approvals are evidence; collect them with dates.
- **Approval chain.** Who can say yes, who can say no, and the escalation order (HR partner, HR manager, employee relations, legal). Draft to the level that can actually decide.
- **Interim measures.** HR can often grant a temporary measure while the formal request is pending. Ask for it in writing, with start date.
- **Documentation HR keeps.** Interactive-process meeting notes, alternatives offered, reasons for denial. Ask for copies; the user's own same-day recap is the backup.
- **Retaliation screen.** Any adverse change after a request (schedule, review rating, discipline, exclusion) gets logged with date and source, not characterized. Motive theories stay out of anything sent.

## Corner 4: Employee

Read the file as its own author, and as an opposing lawyer reading it two years later.

- **Evidence and notice.** The first-request date, every later notice, and who knew what and when. Written beats verbal. Dates from the administrator's own letters are the strongest.
- **Vulnerability.** Overclaims, undocumented labels, contradictions between fields or between forms, earlier forms with No answers (explain why), and motive theories. Statements on disability-benefit applications ("unable to work") must be reconcilable with "qualified with accommodation"; flag any conflict. Name the weak points before anyone else does.
- **Own words.** Capability in the user's terms, function described concretely (what, how long, how often, what happens).
- **Human register.** Plain, dated, confident without grandiosity. Flag passive or softening phrasing ("no worries," "please advise," "sorry to bother"). One ask per message where possible.

## Four-corner pass (default workflow)

1. Inventory the documents in hand. Tag each VERIFIED or RECALL; list what is missing as HOLD.
2. Build the timeline: request, each notice, each status, each decision, each closure, with source and date. Flag the charge deadline.
3. Read each key document and each draft through all four corners. Record findings with the corner named.
4. Resolve conflicts between corners (a sentence the employee corner likes that the law corner flags as an overclaim loses).
5. Produce the outputs the user asked for.

## Outputs

- **Four-corner read:** item | examiner | law | HR | employee | net call. One row per document, field, or draft sentence that matters.
- **Status-conflict table:** item | source A says | source B says | date | what resolves it.
- **Pattern table:** item | approval source and date | reversal source and date | evidence in hand (yes/no).
- **Dated message:** Fact. Document. Ask. By when.
- **Filing checklist:** required fields; truncated fields go to a supplement; screenshot the confirmation with date and time; send a patch message with the claim number; upload supporting documents; set a follow-up date.
- **Evidence tiers:** VERIFIED (source, date, method), INFERENCE, HYPOTHESIS, RECALL, HOLD (could not assess). A HOLD names the missing piece.
